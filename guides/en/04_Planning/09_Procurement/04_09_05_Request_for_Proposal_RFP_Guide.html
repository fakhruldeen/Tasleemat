Tasleemat Forms Guide

Project Artifact: Request for Proposal

Document Reference: PMO-04.09.05

This document provides a comprehensive, professional reference to understand the purpose and effective usage of the Request for Proposal in alignment with the Tasleemat framework.

1. What?

Two sections. The first is what is being bought and who authorised the invitation: the purpose and its authority, the background and objectives, the scope with its boundaries, the deliverables against their dates, the technical requirements with the functional and non-functional kept apart, and the commercial requirements including what a price is expected to contain. The second is what happens to the responses: how they must be submitted and by when, the criteria they will be scored against with their weights, how questions are asked and answered, and what the award commits anyone to.

2. Why?

Because a request for proposal is the only document in which a supplier can find out what it is being judged on. Everything difficult about a procurement dispute comes from the fact that this information was not available at the time: criteria settled after the bids arrived cannot be appealed to, because no supplier saw them, and a clarification given to one bidder is a private amendment to the requirement that the others never saw. A request that omits the weights, the answer rules and the deadline meanings does not simplify the procurement; it moves every one of those arguments to the point where nobody can act on the answer.

3. When?

Once, before responses are invited, and then revised only by re-issuing it, because an amended request that is circulated rather than re-issued is a document whose suppliers hold different versions. The question period closes before responses are opened or priced, since a question answered late is answered to whoever has already committed a number. Read again at award, where the question is whether the winning proposal is the one the published criteria selected, and again at closeout, where it is whether what was bought is what was invited.

4. Who?

Owned by the procurement manager, who holds the procurement and is the only party who can commit the buyer, with the project manager supplying the requirements and schedule context so the scope described is the scope the project is managing. The committee chair signs because the evaluation criteria are a commitment to more than one supplier: they are what the losing suppliers were told they would be judged against, and a chair who has not read them cannot certify that the award followed them.

Tailoring Tips

  • Publish every answer to every supplier. One supplier's clarification is another supplier's change to the requirement, and a private answer is how a contest stops being fair without anybody deciding that it should.
  • Give every criterion a weight and say what a high score looks like. A criterion without a weight is one that can decide the outcome without deciding anything.
  • Close the question period before responses are priced. A question answered late is answered to whoever has already committed a number.
  • State what a price must include. A request that asks for a price without saying what is in it produces responses that cannot be compared, and the cheapest one is then the one that left the most out.

5. How?

To accurately complete the Request for Proposal, populate the following sections based on the project context (ensuring reference to parameters.md for the general project variables):

  • Purpose and Authority: What this request is authorised to invite, and by whom. The authority clause is what makes the contest defensible: it names the instrument that authorised the procurement, so a later reader can tell an invitation the organisation is entitled to issue from a document somebody drafted because they wanted something.
  • Project Overview: The background, the objectives, and the outcome being bought - enough that a supplier who has never heard of the organisation can decide whether this is work they want. A request that describes deliverables without describing the problem produces proposals that are technically compliant and solve something nobody had.
  • Scope of Work: The work and the boundaries of it, described so that two suppliers would propose the same work from this description alone. The exclusions matter as much as the inclusions, because an omission reads as an inclusion and the supplier who priced it that way is the one whose price becomes impossible to deliver.
  • Deliverables and Schedule: What will be handed over and by when, milestone by milestone. A supplier prices the schedule it is given, so a schedule stated only as a total duration invites every supplier to assume its own plan and the evaluation to compare plans that were never compared.
  • Technical Requirements: The functional and non-functional requirements the solution must satisfy, separated because they are evaluated differently: a function that is missing is a gap, whereas a performance target that is missed is a shortfall, and a form that treats them as one list cannot say which of the two has occurred.
  • Commercial Requirements: The pricing structure, the currency, what is included and what is billed separately, and the period the price holds for. A request that asks for a price without saying what it must include produces responses that are not comparable, and the cheapest one is then the one that left the most out.
  • Submission Requirements: How a proposal must be submitted and by when, in a form the buyer can actually open: who may submit, what the deadline means, whether a proposal may be withdrawn before the deadline, and what happens to one that arrives late. Each of these is a point at which a well-meaning supplier can be turned away by accident, and an accident at this point is indistinguishable from a rigged contest.
  • Evaluation Criteria and Weights: The criteria the proposals will be scored against, each with the weight it carries and what a high score looks like. This is the clause that decides whether the contest was real. Criteria published after the bids are in are not criteria but a justification, and no supplier can appeal to them because no supplier saw them; a stated weight also stops a criterion nobody expected from deciding the outcome.
  • Clarifications and Questions: How questions are asked, answered, and published, and the date the answer period closes. Two rules carry this section: every answer goes to every supplier, because an answer given to one is a private amendment to the requirement; and the question period closes before submissions open the work of pricing, because a question answered late is answered to whoever has already committed their number.
  • Award and Contract: How the successful supplier is chosen, what happens next, and what the award does and does not commit anyone to. Naming the route from award to statement of work is what stops the gap between winning and signing from becoming a second negotiation with no rules.
