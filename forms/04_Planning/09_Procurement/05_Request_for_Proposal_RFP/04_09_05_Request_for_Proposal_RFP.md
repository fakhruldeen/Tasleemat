> **Context and Definition:**
> An invitation to suppliers to compete for work, and the document that makes the competition contestable. It states what is being bought, what a supplier must demonstrate, how submissions will be handled, and the criteria each proposal will be scored against with the weight each one carries. The difference between that last part and a justification is the difference between a contest and a decision that was already made.

> **Alignment:**
> The Request for Proposal must be consistent with: the procurement strategy, which decides what is bought and on what terms; the source selection criteria, which the evaluation method must follow rather than restate; and the statement of work, which is drafted from the winning response and cannot contradict what was invited here.

## Invitation and Scope

### Purpose and Authority
**Instructions:** What this request is authorised to invite, and by whom. The authority clause is what makes the contest defensible: it names the instrument that authorised the procurement, so a later reader can tell an invitation the organisation is entitled to issue from a document somebody drafted because they wanted something.

**Instructions:** The background, the objectives, and the outcome being bought - enough that a supplier who has never heard of the organisation can decide whether this is work they want. ### Scope of Work
**Instructions:** The work and the boundaries of it, described so that two suppliers would propose the same work from this description alone. The exclusions matter as much as the inclusions, because an omission reads as an inclusion and the supplier who priced it that way is the one whose price becomes impossible to deliver.

**Instructions:** What will be handed over and by when, milestone by milestone. A supplier prices the schedule it is given, so a schedule stated only as a total duration invites every supplier to assume its own plan and the evaluation to compare plans that were never compared.

## Process and Evaluation

### Submission Requirements
**Instructions:** How a proposal must be submitted and by when, in a form the buyer can actually open: who may submit, what the deadline means, whether a proposal may be withdrawn before the deadline, and what happens to one that arrives late. Each of these is a point at which a well-meaning supplier can be turned away by accident, and an accident at this point is indistinguishable from a rigged contest.

### Evaluation Criteria and Weights
**Instructions:** The criteria the proposals will be scored against, each with the weight it carries and what a high score looks like. This is the clause that decides whether the contest was real. Criteria published after the bids are in are not criteria but a justification, and no supplier can appeal to them because no supplier saw them; a stated weight also stops a criterion nobody expected from deciding the outcome.

### Clarifications and Questions
**Instructions:** How questions are asked, answered, and published, and the date the answer period closes. Two rules carry this section: every answer goes to every supplier, because an answer given to one is a private amendment to the requirement; and the question period closes before submissions open the work of pricing, because a question answered late is answered to whoever has already committed their number.
